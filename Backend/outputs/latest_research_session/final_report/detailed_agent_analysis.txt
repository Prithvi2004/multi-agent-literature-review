[P1] Efficient Transformers: A Survey
   Problem: The quadratic complexity O(n²) of the standard self-attention mechanism limits application to long sequences.
   Method: Survey and categorization of efficient transformer architectures into classes: fixed patterns, learnable patterns, memory-efficient approximations, and low-rank approximations.
   Results: Not Reported (Survey paper, no primary results).
   Limitations: Many efficient approximations trade off global receptive field for computational efficiency, which can harm performance on tasks requiring long-range dependencies. No single method is optimal for all scenarios.

[P2] Linformer: Self-Attention with Linear Complexity
   Problem: The quadratic complexity O(n²) of self-attention with respect to sequence length.
   Method: Projects the key and value matrices into a lower-dimensional space using a fixed projection, reducing complexity to O(n).
   Results: On the MNLI-m task, achieved an accuracy of 84.1% (standard Transformer: 84.6%).
   Limitations: Performance is sensitive to the choice of projection dimension.

[P3] Reformer: The Efficient Transformer
   Problem: High memory and computational cost of standard transformer attention.
   Method: Uses Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) to approximate attention (complexity O(n log n)) and reversible residual layers to reduce memory usage.
   Results: On image generation (Imagenet-64), achieved a bits per dimension (BPD) score of 3.65 (full attention: 3.57).
   Limitations: LSH attention may not be as effective for tasks requiring very precise long-range dependencies.

[P4] MobileBERT: Task-Agnostic Compression of BERT
   Problem: Large size and computational cost of BERT.
   Method: Uses a bottleneck structure and stacked feed-forward networks. Trained via layer-by-layer knowledge distillation from a teacher BERT.
   Results: On the GLUE benchmark, average score of 77.0 (teacher BERT: 78.3). On QQP, F1 score of 71.2.
   Limitations: Performance is highly dependent on the quality of the teacher model and the distillation process.

[P5] DistilBERT, a distilled version of BERT
   Problem: Large size of BERT models.
   Method: Knowledge distillation during pre-training; student model is trained to mimic the outputs of the full BERT model. Has 40% fewer parameters.
   Results: On GLUE, retained 97% of BERT's performance (average score 76.6 vs. 79.0). On SST-2, accuracy of 91.3%.
   Limitations: Distillation can lead to a loss of nuanced linguistic knowledge captured by the larger model.

[P6] ALBERT: A Lite BERT for Self-supervised Learning
   Problem: Large parameter size of BERT models.
   Method: Factorized embedding parameterization (separating embedding size from hidden size) and cross-layer parameter sharing.
   Results: On GLUE, ALBERT-base average score 80.1 (BERT-base: 79.0). On RACE, accuracy of 67.4%.
   Limitations: Parameter sharing might limit the model's representational capacity, potentially leading to a performance ceiling.

[P7] Transformer Quality in Linear Time (FLASH)
   Problem: Quadratic cost of standard attention for long sequences.
   Method: Gated attention unit combined with a linear recurrence, achieving linear complexity O(n).
   Results: On PG-19 language modeling, perplexity of 22.4 (Transformer-XL: 22.1).
   Limitations: The gating mechanism may not capture complex dependencies as effectively as standard attention in all cases.

[P8] A Survey on Efficient Training of Transformers
   Problem: High computational cost of pre-training large transformers.
   Method: Survey of efficient training techniques, including dynamic activation pruning, mixed-precision training, and gradient checkpointing.
   Results: Notes that gradient checkpointing can reduce memory usage by over 50% during training.
   Limitations: Increased complexity of implementing and tuning these methods compared to standard training.

[P9] TinyBERT: Distilling BERT for Natural Language Understanding
   Problem: Compression of BERT for efficient deployment.
   Method: Two-stage distillation framework: general distillation during pre-training, then task-specific distillation during fine-tuning.
   Results: On GLUE, 4-layer model average score 75.5 (~96% of BERT-base). On MRPC, F1 score of 88.5.
   Limitations: The framework's complexity requires careful orchestration of the two distillation phases.

[P11] FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention
   Problem: High memory usage of standard attention implementations.
   Method: IO-aware exact attention algorithm using tiling and recomputation to minimize GPU memory reads/writes.
   Results: Achieved up to 3x faster training times for BERT-large on sequences of length 512.
   Limitations: Speedup is most significant for long sequences and is hardware-dependent.

[P13] Prune Once for All: Sparse Pre-Trained Language Models
   Problem: Large size of pre-trained transformers.
   Method: Applies magnitude pruning once to a pre-trained model to create a sparse mask; the pruned model is fine-tuned for multiple tasks.
   Results: On SQuAD v1.1, pruned BERT-base (80% sparsity) F1 score 85.2 (dense model: 88.5).
   Limitations: The pruning mask is fixed after initial pruning, which may not be optimal for all downstream tasks.

[P16] A Comprehensive Survey on Transformer Compression
   Problem: Large size and computational demand of pre-trained models like BERT.
   Method: Survey of compression techniques: pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, low-rank factorization.
   Results: Notes quantization (e.g., 8-bit) can reduce model size by 75% with minimal accuracy loss (<1% drop on GLUE).
   Limitations: Aggressive compression often leads to a performance drop; optimal technique depends on target hardware.

[P18] Efficient Long-Range Transformers: A Survey of Techniques and Benchmarks
   Problem: Poor scalability of transformers to very long sequences (>4000 tokens).
   Method: Survey and benchmarking of models like Longformer and Performer on long-range tasks (e.g., LRA benchmark).
   Results: Reports models like Performer can achieve accuracy >80% on the LRA Pathfinder task efficiently.
   Limitations: Lack of a standardized benchmark for long-range tasks makes fair comparisons difficult.

[P19] TinyBERT Revisited: A Scalable Knowledge Distillation Framework
   Problem: Scaling knowledge distillation to larger teacher models and more tasks.
   Method: Improved TinyBERT framework with scalable distillation (for larger teachers) and enhanced distillation loss function.
   Results: On GLUE, 6-layer model average score 79.2 (teacher: 82.1). On CoLA, Matthew's correlation coefficient 57.3.
   Limitations: Increased computational cost of distilling from very large teachers.

[P22] MobileBERT-v2: An Improved Compact BERT with Enhanced Training Techniques
   Problem: Improving upon the original MobileBERT.
   Method: Incorporates improved training recipe: advanced data augmentation and modified distillation objective.
   Results: On GLUE, average score 79.3 (original MobileBERT: 77.0, BERT-base: 79.0). On MNLI-m, accuracy 84.2%.
   Limitations: Gains are sensitive to the specific augmentation techniques used.

# Lightweight Transformer Models for Natural Language Processing: A Comprehensive Literature Review

## Abstract

The transformer architecture has revolutionized natural language processing, but its computational demands—particularly the quadratic complexity of self-attention—have spurred intensive research into lightweight alternatives. This review synthesizes the evolution of efficient transformer research from 2019-2023, covering architectural modifications, compression techniques, and optimization methods. We identify three dominant paradigms: attention mechanism optimization (e.g., Linformer [P2], Reformer [P3]), model compression via knowledge distillation and pruning (DistilBERT [P5], MobileBERT [P4]), and parameter efficiency through factorization (ALBERT [P6]). Recent surveys [P1, P8, P16, P18] reveal that while significant progress has been made—with some methods achieving near-linear complexity—fundamental trade-offs persist between computational efficiency, model capacity, and task performance. The field is converging toward hybrid approaches that combine multiple efficiency strategies, though no universal solution has emerged.

## 1. Introduction & Motivation

The transformer architecture's self-attention mechanism, while powerful, exhibits O(n²) complexity with respect to sequence length, making it computationally prohibitive for long sequences and resource-constrained environments [P1]. This limitation has driven sustained research into lightweight transformers that maintain competitive performance while reducing computational costs. The motivation spans practical deployment needs (mobile devices, edge computing) and theoretical interest in understanding which aspects of transformer architecture are essential for performance.

Early work focused primarily on reducing parameter counts through distillation [P5] and parameter sharing [P6], while subsequent research targeted the attention mechanism itself [P2, P3]. More recent approaches combine multiple strategies [P4, P22] and address specialized challenges like long-range dependencies [P7, P18]. The evolution reflects a deepening understanding of transformer internals and a recognition that efficiency requires holistic optimization rather than isolated improvements.
